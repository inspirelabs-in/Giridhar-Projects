01 · System Prompt

The Charter: Our System Prompt

AI Labs is a product and R&D team inside Inspirelabs. Not an internal IT function. Not a feature factory. Not a research group that publishes and moves on. We exist to turn AI capability into things of real value, and that value lands in two equally serious places.

The mandate comes straight from the CEO: build AI products, agents, and systems that matter. Some make the company dramatically faster and smarter from the inside. Some go out as standalone products the wider world pays for. Neither is the "real" work and neither is the leftover. They're parallel tracks of one mission, and you'll be expected to move on both.

Mandate 1: Internal leverage

Build the tools and systems that let Inspirelabs run at a speed and intelligence competitors can't match. Automate the manual work. Turn raw data into answers. Give every team a capability it didn't have yesterday. The whole ecosystem is our proving ground, and GrabOn is the richest, first arena within it: real users, real data, real stakes.

Mandate 2: External products

Build AI products that stand on their own in the market. A tool proven inside the company can become a product others buy; some ideas get built for the world from day one. This is how AI Labs becomes a venture engine instead of a cost center, and how it fills the Services revenue stream that sits near zero today.

What winning looks like

Things that shipped and created measurable value: hours saved, revenue recovered, decisions accelerated, or a product people adopt and pay for. A demo that dazzles in a meeting and never ships isn't a milestone. It's a failure, on either track. We are judged on what's live, not what's promising.

★ The bar is public

The CEO's reference points are explicit: agents running companies 24/7, the "Company Brain" architecture (YC's 2026 Request for Startups), Karpathy-style LLM Wikis. We're not trying to catch the market, we're trying to build at the frontier of it, for ourselves and for customers.

That's the mandate. The obvious next question is how anyone knows whether we delivered on it, and for that there's a single number.

02 · System Prompt

The Reward Signal: 5 Shipped Products

That number is on the company's scoreboard, in black and white: five products, live and earning revenue this year. Not five prototypes. Not five pilots. Not "shipped to production" and quietly left there. Five products that sell. We'll build dozens of features, automations, and skills along the way, and any one of them can become a product the moment we package it and someone pays. But the five are what we're measured on: things that earn.

This isn't a side goal: it's a company scoreboard line

Inspirelabs runs a 12-month operating doctrine called Win The Year, built to take the company up by roughly half again, a step-change in revenue over the year, not a stretch on the old trajectory. It has a public scoreboard of eight outcomes the whole company plays the same game on, published every Monday without exception. One of those eight, "AI-led products / features shipped, in production", belongs to us. AI Labs doesn't sit beside the company's goals. It owns one of the eight. That's the scoreboard's wording; our internal bar is tougher and simpler, a product only counts when it's earning revenue, not merely live.

What counts: and what doesn't

A product is anything packaged and sold: something a customer pays for, internally or in the market. An agent can be a product. So can a skill, an automation, or an API, if you package it and someone pays to use it. What doesn't count is the same artifact sitting unsold in our repo, that's a building block, not a product. The line isn't the artifact type. It's whether it earns revenue. A thing in production that nobody pays for scores zero here, the same way a demo does.

The company's real bottleneck: and where we fit

Win The Year is blunt that the thing slowing the company isn't the market or the technology, it's the calendar: too many decisions routed through one person. That's being fixed by giving managers real authority, not by us. Our job is to remove the other drag, the manual work, the data with no synthesis, the hours spent producing instead of deciding, so every team gets its time back. (The full diagnosis, and the search-shift arena we build against, is in §16.)

The Weights: What We Optimize For

Knowing the target is one thing; knowing how to choose when the path to it is unclear is another. These are the decision rules we fall back on when the right move isn't obvious, the weights behind every judgment call. Values aren't posters here. When in doubt, re-read these; they break ties.

01

Value or it didn't happen

Every single thing we build must create measurable value, for a GrabOn team (recovered revenue, eliminated hours, reduced risk, a faster decision) or for a customer in the market (a product they adopt and pay for). "It's technically impressive" is not value. "It's interesting" is not value. If you can't name who is better off and by how much, inside or outside, stop and rethink the build.

→ Test: name the beneficiary and the number, on either track.

02

Think first, then ship fast

Deep research and documentation come before code, always. Understand the problem, study how it's solved, ask many questions, write the spec in full. Do that and the build becomes fast and easy. The mistake is vibe-coding your way to a demo; the discipline is doing the hard thinking upstream so the building downstream is quick. A working V1 that came from a real spec beats a fast hack that solved the wrong problem.

→ Test: could someone build it from your doc, before you code?

03

Own the outcome, not the task

Your job is not "I wrote the agent." It's "the metric moved." Ownership means you chase the result through QA, adoption, and the messy last mile where most automation quietly dies. You don't hand off a half-finished thing and call it done.

→ Test: did the number actually change?

04

Initiative is the baseline

The roadmap is visible and the priorities are published. So the next thing to do is almost always knowable without being told. Proposing the plan is the expected behavior; asking "what should I do?" when the board is full is the exception that needs a reason.

→ Test: did you bring a proposal, or a question?

05

Write it down

Knowledge that lives only in your head is a liability to the team. Everything non-trivial goes into the Wiki in a form an agent, or the next hire, can act on. If a teammate can't rebuild your work from what you wrote, you're not finished.

→ Test: could someone rebuild this without asking you?

06

Think in revenue

We are a business, not a lab that happens to be inside one. Tie every build to a revenue stream and a challenge. The most elegant agent that touches no stream and solves no real problem is a hobby project we can't afford.

→ Test: which stream does this move?

07

Challenge bad specs

If an ask is wrong, vague, or aimed at the wrong problem, say so and propose a better version. Silently building the wrong thing wastes everyone's time; stalling because the spec is imperfect wastes it differently. Push back with a proposal.

→ Test: did you improve the ask, or just execute it?

Stay on the frontier

The tooling shifts weekly. Knowing the latest model, technique, or tool is part of the job, not extra credit. Bring what you find to the team. The day we stop being current is the day we stop being AI Labs.

10 · Domain Context

How Search Actually Works

Almost all of GrabOn's revenue starts with one thing: someone typing into Google and clicking our result instead of a competitor's. If you understand nothing else about the business, understand this chain, because nearly every internal build either protects it, measures it, or tries to recover it. No SEO background needed; we'll build it up from zero.

The five words you'll hear constantly

These are the metrics the whole search operation runs on. Plain definitions first:

TermWhat it actually means
KeywordWhat a person types into Google. For us, that's things like "myntra coupons", "dominos promo code", "ajio offers". Each keyword is a tiny market: some are huge (millions of searches), some are tiny, and the valuable ones are where buying intent is highest.
ImpressionYour page showed up in someone's search results for a keyword, whether or not they clicked. If we rank for "nykaa coupons" and 100k people search it, that's up to 100k impressions. Impressions measure visibility.
Ranking (position)Where your link sits on the results page. Position 1 is the top organic result; position 10 is the bottom of page one; page two might as well not exist. Ranking is the single biggest driver of everything downstream.
CTR (click-through rate)Of the people who saw you (impressions), what fraction clicked. Clicks ÷ impressions. Position 1 might get a large share of clicks; position 8 gets a tiny one. CTR is how visibility turns into actual visitors.
Bounce rateOf the people who landed on your page, what fraction left without doing anything, no click, no reveal, no journey onward. High bounce = the page didn't deliver what the search promised.
Avg. session timeHow long people actually stay and engage. Longer, engaged sessions signal a useful page; instant exits signal a mismatch. Google watches these engagement signals when it decides who deserves to rank.

How they chain into revenue

Here's the part that matters: these aren't separate numbers, they're a pipeline where each stage multiplies the next. Read it as one equation:

KEYWORD people search it │ ▼ RANKING decides how often we appear ──▶ IMPRESSIONS │ ▼ CTR decides how many of those clicks ──▶ VISITORS │ ▼ page quality (low BOUNCE, good SESSION) keeps them moving │ ▼ they reveal a code, go to the merchant, buy │ ▼ REVENUE (CPS / Delivery)

Because it multiplies, a weakness at any stage caps everything after it. The best keyword in the world earns nothing if we rank position 9 (few impressions convert to clicks). A great ranking earns nothing if the page bounces everyone straight back. This is why "rankings" and "revenue" are almost the same conversation here, move a page from position 5 to position 2 on a high-intent keyword and you've multiplied impressions × CTR at the same time, which is why it shows up directly in the money.

Why a small ranking change is a big deal

Click share falls off a cliff with position, the top one or two results take the large majority of clicks, and everything below page one is rounding error. So recovering a single high-value keyword from position 6 to position 2 isn't a 4-step nudge; it can multiply the traffic (and revenue) from that keyword several times over. This is the entire thesis behind the SERP loss-recovery work (§20, §2).

Why engagement metrics feed back

Bounce rate and session time aren't just outcomes, they're inputs to future rankings. Google promotes pages people engage with and quietly demotes pages they bounce from. So a page that loads slowly or buries the coupon doesn't just lose today's user; it slowly loses its ranking, which loses tomorrow's impressions. The funnel and the ranking are the same system.

GrabOn's reality, and why it's worth protecting

GrabOn's traffic engine was built over a decade, category by category, into thousands of high-intent keywords where we rank at or near the top. That's the moat. But it rests on one assumption: that Google keeps sending the clicks. Two things now threaten that, and they're the reason AI Labs exists on the Track A side:

AI Overviews answer before the click

Google increasingly answers the query on the results page itself (AI Overviews / SGE). The impression still happens, but the click doesn't, so CTR collapses even when our ranking is unchanged. We can hold position 1 and still lose the visit. This is the existential pressure on the whole model (§16).

The funnel leak after the click

Even when we win the click, most users who reach the reveal step don't make it to the merchant (the ~1.4% go-to-merchant reality, §9). So we lose revenue twice: some before the click (AI Overviews) and most after it (the funnel). Both are measurable, and both are exactly where an agent can move a number.

◆ What this means for what you build

When you touch anything SEO-adjacent, you now know the levers: keyword value, ranking position, CTR, and on-page engagement, and that they multiply. A build that lifts CTR on high-intent pages, recovers slipping rankings, or cuts bounce on the reveal page isn't "an SEO nicety", it's pulling directly on the largest revenue lever in the company. The data to do this is real and queryable in our analytics warehouse; you don't have to take any of this on faith.

How Sales Actually Works

If Search is how GrabOn earns from users, Sales is how it earns from brands, the deals, the commercial terms, the relationships that turn traffic into paid partnerships. Most AI builders have never worked a sales pipeline, so here it is from zero: the words you'll hear, what they mean, and how they connect to money.

The vocabulary, plainly

TermWhat it actually means
LeadA potential customer, here, usually a brand we could sign (for a branding deal, a direct partnership, or a service). A lead is just a name with potential; it isn't money yet. Leads Agent (§2) exists to find and qualify these.
PipelineAll the leads in progress, staged by how close they are to closing (e.g. contacted → in talks → negotiating → closed). "Pipeline of ₹X" means the total potential value of open deals, not money in the bank.
Conversion rate (CR)What fraction of leads actually become paying deals. 100 leads, 5 closes = 5% CR. It's the efficiency of the whole motion: a small CR improvement on a big pipeline is large money. (Note: CR also describes user purchase conversion on-site, same word, two contexts, watch which one's meant.)
Top lineRevenue, the money coming in, before costs. "Grow the top line" = sell more.
Bottom lineProfit, what's left after costs. You can grow the top line and shrink the bottom line if a deal is expensive to deliver. A bootstrapped, profitable company watches both.
IO / dealThe actual signed agreement (an "insertion order" for a branding/campaign deal). Closing an IO is the moment a lead becomes top-line revenue.

The brand-side money streams

Recall the four revenue streams (§8). Three of them are sold or managed through commercial relationships:

CPS / Affiliate

The brand pays a cut of each sale we drive. Often runs through an affiliate network (a middleman platform like the ones tracked in Trackier) rather than a direct contract, the network sits between us and the brand, tracks the sales, and handles payouts.

Branding

Brands pay for placement and visibility, sponsored listings, featured spots. This is sold directly by Sales as deals/IOs and renewed over time. Higher-margin and more predictable than pure affiliate.

Delivery

Sales driven through external publishers / partners (§8), managed as campaigns with budgets, targets, and retained margins. They run campaigns that drive sales; we earn on what's delivered. Quality and validation matter, a partner that delivers junk doesn't renew.

How a deal actually moves, and where the numbers live

Campaigns

A live commercial arrangement with a brand, a budget to spend, a target to hit (sales, clicks, installs), a window of time. "Paused campaign" = stopped, usually because targets slipped or budget ran out; reactivating paused campaigns is real recoverable revenue.

Affiliate networks

Platforms that connect publishers (us) to advertisers (brands) and track conversions. Tools like Trackier record who drove which sale so payouts are correct. When networks and direct deals overlap, attribution gets messy, which is exactly where errors (and recoverable money) hide.

Validation data

Brands don't pay for every reported sale, they validate first (was it real? returned? within terms?). The gap between reported and validated is where revenue leaks or gets disputed. Getting validation right is unglamorous and worth a lot.

Client calls

The relationship layer: QBRs (quarterly business reviews), renewals, escalations, "what would you pay more for?" These conversations decide retention and upsell, and they generate signals (what's working, what's not) that a good system should capture, not lose.

◆ Why a builder should care

Every one of these has an AI lever. Finding and scoring leads (Leads Agent). Lifting CR by prioritizing the right leads and arming reps. Catching validation gaps automatically. Turning client-call notes into structured signals. The Services stream sits near zero today (§8), which means the sales motion is one of the most under-automated, highest-upside surfaces in the whole company.

The Objective Function: How We Score Value

You now know the business: how it earns, where it leaks, who runs it. The question this section answers is what to do with that knowledge, how we decide whether a build is worth your week. This is the heart of the document. "Value or it didn't happen" is the first value (§3); here it becomes a method you can actually run, not just a slogan. Before you build anything, put it through this.

The kinds of value we create

Every worthwhile build creates at least one. The first four are internal value (Track A); the fifth is external value (Track B). The best builds create several, and a build that delivers internal value and then becomes a product delivers both.

① Revenue captured internal

Recovers or grows money we'd otherwise lose or never earn. Stale-coupon recovery (CPS), funnel fixes (Go-To-Merchant), monetizing dead-traffic pages. The most direct internal value.

② Time eliminated internal

Removes manual hours so humans do higher-leverage work. The synthesis dashboard that ends manual report-stitching. Leads generation that replaces hand-research. Measured in hours/week reclaimed.

③ Risk reduced internal

Prevents costly errors, expired codes shown to users, compliance slips, brand-keyword violations, missed escalations. Harder to measure but real; quantify the avoided cost.

④ Decisions accelerated internal

Turns raw data into a trusted answer faster. The synthesis layer the org lacks. Better decisions, sooner, with more confidence, the Company Brain thesis in miniature.

⑤ Market value created external

A product, skill, agent, or API that a customer outside Inspirelabs adopts and pays for. Value here is measured the way any product is: adoption, retention, revenue. This is Track B (§26), and it counts every bit as much as the four above.

The value test: answer these before building

#QuestionIf you can't answer…
1Which track? Internal (a GrabOn team) or external (a market)?…you don't yet know what you're building.
2Who is better off? Name the team, person, or customer.…you're solving an imaginary problem.
3By how much? A number or a tight range.…you can't prove it worked later.
4What's the baseline? The "before" state.…you have no proof of impact.
5Could it travel? If internal, could it cross to a product? If external, is the ecosystem a proving ground first?…fine, but note it; many builds travel (§26).
▲ The discipline

This isn't bureaucracy, it's what separates AI Labs from every team that built impressive demos nobody used. Two minutes. If the build survives the test on either track, it's worth your week. If it doesn't, you just saved your week.

The Loss Landscape: Where the Biggest Opportunities Are

The value test tells you how to judge a build. This section tells you where the high-value builds actually are. It's the Track A arena, the internal terrain, and one framing note up front: the company's own Win The Year diagnosis is that its ceiling has been the calendar, decisions bottlenecked through one person. That's being fixed by giving managers authority, not by us. What's below is different. It's the terrain AI Labs builds against: the search shift, the data drag, the revenue leaks. Not why the Labs exists, but where its highest-value internal work lives, and often the first step to a product that travels to Track B (§26).

Force 1: The search-and-agent shift (the big one)

The coupon-aggregator model is simple: rank in search → a human browses → they grab a coupon → we earn affiliate revenue. That chain is being reshaped by AI at every link:

AI Overviews / AI Mode

Google answers deal queries directly in the results. Users never click through. Some retailers report ~30% organic traffic declines. Every point of CTR lost is meaningful annual CPS gone.

Google Universal Cart

Google is building the deal-and-checkout layer itself, competing directly with GrabOn's core discovery-and-redirect function, from inside the search box.

Autonomous shopping agents

Gemini Spark, Perplexity Comet, and OpenAI's agents shop for the user. They skip the human browsing behaviour the whole model depends on. No human eyeballs, no coupon page, no affiliate click.

⚠ Read this twice

This is not a SEO slump to optimize around. It is a structural change in how people find and buy things. The destination-for-humans business has a shrinking runway. The companies that survive make themselves useful to the AI systems instead of competing with them for human attention. The strategic window to build that is roughly 6–9 months, while competitors are still debating whether it's real.

The counter-move: become infrastructure, not a destination

The response, and the most important strategic idea in this document, is to stop fighting for the human's click and instead become the source the machine cites: expose GrabOn's coupon and merchant data as agent-accessible infrastructure so that when an AI agent needs "the best offer for X," it pulls from us. That's the North Star, and §17 lays out exactly what it means and how it's built.

Force 2: The synthesis vacuum (self-perpetuating)

Here is a fact that explains half of the daily pain in this company: reports@grabon.in auto-emits "Dynamic Coupon Codes Data" twice a day, it has fired over 700 times, blasting raw merchant/coupon tables to team mailing lists. So the org has plenty of automated data emission. What it has none of is a synthesis layer that turns that data into an answer.

⚠ Our highest-leverage internal target

The number the CEO asks for daily is assembled by hand. Revenue is tallied manually. There is no dashboard, no automated revenue report, no data→answer layer. So a human becomes the integration layer, pulling, stitching, reconciling, time that should be spent deciding, not assembling. This sits right alongside the company's calendar problem: both are about people doing by hand what a system should do. Building that missing synthesis layer is the single highest-leverage thing AI Labs can do internally, and it gives every team back hours, which is exactly the OPP lever Win The Year is built on.

19 · Search Space

What's Worth Solving, And How We Pick

The map gives you more candidates than you can build at once. Two questions narrow it: is this even the right kind of task to automate, and if several qualify, which one first?

What makes a task worth automating

✓ Good candidates

Repetitive and frequent · clear inputs and outputs · rule-driven or pattern-driven · currently eating human hours · tied to a revenue stream · low cost of a reversible mistake.

✕ Poor candidates (for now)

One-off tasks · pure human judgment with no signal · irreversible actions with no checkpoint · tasks where the data doesn't exist yet · things where a mistake is catastrophic and unrecoverable.

The reframe that matters: don't ask "can AI do this?", almost everything has an AI angle. Ask "should we automate this first?" The constraint is never capability; it's prioritization. So when several qualify, score them. We use a simple ICE-style lens adapted for AI Labs, fast enough to do in your head, rigorous enough to defend.

FactorAskScore 1–5
ImpactHow much value (revenue/time/risk/decision) if it works?5 = moves a stream
ConfidenceHow sure are we it'll work and get adopted?5 = near-certain
EaseHow fast can a V1 ship? Is the data available?5 = days, data ready
LeverageDoes it compound, reusable, externally sellable, unblocks other builds?5 = highly reusable

Multiply or sum, then sort. Bias toward high-Ease, high-Confidence builds early, shipping a real V1 fast builds trust and teaches you the domain faster than a six-week moonshot. Save the big bets for when you've earned context.

★ The adoption multiplier

The most common reason automation fails here isn't the code, it's that nobody uses it. Weight Confidence of adoption heavily. An 80%-accurate agent people trust and run beats a 99% agent that sits unused because the owner wasn't brought along. Build with the team that'll use it.

The thinking lenses we apply

Before scoring, interrogate the problem with these. They're how we find the real target instead of the obvious one.

Pareto (the 20%)

Which 20% of this creates 80% of the value? Automate that slice first; ignore the long tail until the core proves out.

Theory of Constraints

What is the ONE bottleneck? Improving anything that isn't the constraint feels productive and changes nothing. Find the constraint, aim there.

Leverage analysis

One-time fix or compounding one? A template saves time once; a system that runs every day saves more every week. Always prefer compounding.

Inversion

Don't only ask "what should we build?" Ask "what should the team STOP doing entirely?" The removals are often bigger wins than the additions.

First principles

When you hear "that's just how it works here," break it to base parts. Why does this process exist? Is the assumption under it still true? Often it isn't.

Jobs-to-be-done

What's the real job this task is doing for the person? Build for the job, not the surface request, the request is usually a guess at a solution.

Quantify the pain: always

Vague pain doesn't drive action; quantified pain is undeniable. The moment someone describes a manual task, put a number on it. This is how you make the case for a build, and how you prove its value after.

★ How to use this section

Playbook 01 · for the SEO team

SERP Loss-Recovery Engine

Turns a raw rankings crawl into a prioritized, owner-assigned recovery list.
Pain
The SEO team gets giant rankings crawls (8,000+ keywords per market) and no time to turn them into action. Lost positions sit undiagnosed. We already know ~67% of lost #1s went to direct brands (Dominos, Agoda, Uber) and ~31% to coupon competitors led by Desidime, and most losses are a single drop to #2, i.e. winnable.
Build
An agent that ingests the crawl, computes date-over-date movement, classifies each loss (to a brand vs. a competitor vs. AIO), and outputs a ranked recovery list, biggest, most-winnable losses first, with the likely cause and a suggested fix per row.
Steps
  1. Ingest the multi-date crawl; dedupe keywords across dates; confirm position #1 mapping.
  2. Compute movement per keyword; flag drops, especially single-position #1→#2 losses on high-value terms.
  3. Classify the winner: direct brand, coupon competitor, or AI Overview taking the slot.
  4. Score by value × winnability; output a ranked sheet with cause + suggested action per row.
  5. Hand SEO a one-screen "fix these ten first" list, not a 8,000-row dump.
Proof
Single-position recoveries on high-value terms; lost-#1 count trending down week over week.
Externalize
Any SEO team buried in rank-tracker exports has this exact problem, a clean "loss-recovery" report is a sellable skill.
Playbook 02 · for Client Services (CS)

Merchant Daily-Tracking Conversion Engine

Drives the company-wide push to move every merchant to daily tracking, and keeps it self-running.
Pain
Moving merchants to daily tracking is a slow, manual, person-by-person chase across the CS POCs (Yamini, Meena, Svati). Progress is uneven, some POCs close fast, others stall with soft language and no urgency, and the lead (Giridhar) ends up chasing it personally instead of it running itself.
Build
A self-updating CS scorecard + nudge system: it tracks each POC's brands and status, flags overdue P1/P2 conversions, and surfaces exactly which brands need escalation, so the weekly review runs on data, not on the lead's memory.
Steps
  1. Ingest the tracking-methods sheet + revenue brands; clean duplicate brand entries (a known data issue).
  2. Filter to P1/P2 brands not yet on daily; assign each to its POC.
  3. Auto-generate a per-POC focus list with status (Converted / In-progress / Overdue).
  4. Flag escalation cases (e.g. high-value brands stuck) for the lead's direct involvement.
  5. Refresh weekly into a one-page dashboard; the rhythm holds without anyone chasing.
Proof
% of merchants on daily tracking climbing toward >80%; weekly review self-running; lead stops being the engine.
Externalize
A "partner-tracking conversion" dashboard generalizes to any affiliate/partnerships team.
Playbook 03 · for SEO / Link-building

Backlink Target & Outreach Engine

Finds real, winnable link targets and writes the outreach, no generic spray.
Pain
Generic backlink advice (guest posts, forums, Quora) is useless, competitors and forums will never link to us. What works is specific: dead competitor pages, blogs that mention a brand but link to no aggregator, a clear value exchange. Finding those by hand is slow.
Build
An agent that, for a target page (e.g. a store's coupons page), finds broken/expired competitor pages (404/410 targets), blogs that discuss the brand but link nowhere, and competitor backlink gaps, then drafts a tailored outreach email per target type, each built around why they'd say yes.
Steps
  1. Crawl for dead competitor pages on the brand (verify 404/410/timeout), broken-link targets convert 15–25%.
  2. Find editorial pages (credit-card blogs, travel/NRI sites, niche blogs) that mention the brand but link to no coupon aggregator.
  3. Pull competitor backlink profiles to find link-gap targets.
  4. For each target type, draft outreach framed on the value exchange, reciprocal DR link, deal data, or writing the update paragraph for them.
  5. Output a verified target sheet + ready-to-send templates; never contact OTAs, Quora, Reddit, or guest-post farms.
Proof
Referring domains up on target pages; outreach reply/placement rate by target type.
Externalize
A "verified backlink targets + outreach" engine is a clean SaaS for any SEO/affiliate shop.
Playbook 04 · for the CEO & leadership

The Synthesis Dashboard

Kills the manual data-stitching that traps the Founder's Office, the highest-leverage internal build.
Pain
The org emits raw data twice daily (reports@grabon.in, 700+ times) but the revenue number the CEO wants is assembled by hand. There's no data→answer layer, so a human becomes the integration layer, the exact trap described in §16.
Build
An ingestion + synthesis layer that consumes the raw coupon/merchant dumps and other feeds, and emits the answers leadership actually asks for, a trusted daily revenue view, merchant performance, ranking-to-revenue links, automatically, in one place.
Steps
  1. Capture the raw feeds at the source (the twice-daily dumps, delivery reports, rankings data).
  2. Model them into a clean store (a simple schema; this has been prototyped against real GrabOn formats).
  3. Build the synthesis: the daily revenue number, top movers, and the questions the CEO repeats.
  4. Surface it as one dashboard leadership trusts, no manual tally, no spreadsheet stitching.
  5. Automate refresh; document the pipeline so it isn't you running it by hand.
Proof
The daily revenue number is no longer assembled by hand; hours/week of manual reporting eliminated; a leader relies on it.
Externalize
Every data-rich company has this gap. A "raw-feeds → trusted dashboard" synthesis layer is a strong product.
▲ The pattern across all four

Each takes a team's worst recurring manual job, turns it into something an agent does reliably, and hands the team back a clean answer instead of raw data. That's the AI Labs move in miniature, and every one of them, proven internally, becomes a product (§26). When you finish onboarding, picking one of these to run is often the fastest path to a visible win.

Pre-Training: Research & Docs Before Code

Before you pick up that first build, read this section twice. It corrects the single most common, and most expensive, wrong assumption people bring to AI Labs: that the job is to sit down, vibe-code, and a working product falls out the other end. It doesn't. That's not how anything good here gets built.

⚠ The mindset to unlearn

"I'll just start building and figure it out as I go." This feels productive and fast. It is neither. It produces agents that solve the wrong problem, miss the data they needed, skip the human checkpoint, and quietly fail in production, after you've spent a week on them. The code was never the hard part.

The actual sequence

The valuable, difficult work happens before any code. When you do it properly, the build itself becomes almost mechanical, fast and easy, because every hard decision is already made. Get this order right and you'll out-ship people who started coding on day one.

Understand the problem deeply

Not the surface ask, the real problem underneath it. Who has it, how often, what it actually costs, what they do today instead. Most "build me X" requests are a guess at a solution; your job is to find the problem behind the guess.

Research relentlessly

How is this solved elsewhere? What data exists and where does it live? What's the system around it? What have we already built that's close? Research is not procrastination, it's the work. The hour you spend here saves the week you'd waste later.

Ask many questions

Ask the data owner, ask the person with the pain, ask Giridhar, ask the model. The number of good questions you ask before building is the single best predictor of whether the build will work. Asking is a strength here, not a weakness, after you've done your own thinking and you're bringing sharp questions, not vague ones.

Write the documentation

Document the problem, the inputs, the logic, the edge cases, the checkpoints, the success metric, in full, before you build. This is the agent spec (§23) made serious. A complete, well-researched spec is 80% of the work; the code is the remaining 20% and it's the easy 20%.

Then build: fast

Now you build, and now "speed over polish on V1" applies cleanly, because you're executing a clear plan instead of discovering the plan through trial and error. This is where vibe-coding-style speed is an asset, downstream of the thinking, never as a replacement for it.

▲ How "ship fast" and "document first" fit together

They're not in tension, they're sequential. Documentation and research come first and they're slow and rigorous. Building comes second and it's fast and loose. People who confuse the order, building fast instead of thinking first, produce demos that don't ship. People who get the order right produce products that work on the first real try.

★ The test

Before you write code, you should be able to hand your documentation to another engineer and have them build roughly the same thing without talking to you. If you can't, you haven't researched and documented enough, keep going. The keyboard can wait.

Context Engineering: Using Your Tools Well

Owning Claude Code or any frontier tool isn't a skill, using it well is, and it's one of the highest-leverage things you'll learn here. Two engineers with identical tools produce wildly different output. The difference is almost entirely in how they prompt, context, and direct the model.

What effective use actually looks like

Context is everything

The model is only as good as what you give it. Feed it the research, the documentation, the real data, the constraints, the examples. A vague prompt gets a vague result; a richly-contexted prompt gets something you can ship. This is exactly why §24 comes first, the documentation you write is the context you'll feed the tool.

Specify the output

Tell it the format, the structure, the length, the tone, the edge cases. "Write the agent" is weak. "Write a Python agent that reads X, applies these rules, flags (never removes), outputs a ranked list as JSON, and handles these three edge cases" is strong.

Iterate deliberately

Don't accept the first output and don't start over from scratch when it's 80% right. Steer: "good, now handle the empty case," "tighten this," "you missed the checkpoint." Direction beats regeneration.

Use examples & negatives

Show it a good example and a bad one. Tell it what not to do. Models latch onto patterns, give them the right pattern to latch onto.

Break big asks down

Don't ask for the whole system in one shot. Decompose: research, then spec, then one module, then the next. Each step is more controllable and easier to verify than a monolith.

Verify, never trust blindly

The tool is confident even when it's wrong. Check outputs against reality, run the code, test the edge cases, confirm the data. Effective use includes healthy skepticism of what the model hands back.

▲ The compounding skill

Prompting and context-engineering get dramatically better with deliberate practice. Keep a running note of what worked. Share your best patterns with the team, and check the Wiki for theirs. The team that prompts well out-builds the team that just has the same tools.

★ Why this ties back to research-first

Notice the through-line: effective tool use depends on good context, and good context comes from the research and documentation you did first (§24). You cannot prompt your way out of not understanding the problem. The thinking upstream is what makes the tool downstream powerful.

24 · Training Loop

Guardrails: Human-In-The-Loop

Speed is the culture, but it never overrides safety on irreversible actions. These are non-negotiable.

Agents flag; humans approve

Any destructive or irreversible action, removing a coupon, deleting data, sending an external email, changing a setting, requires a human decision. The agent surfaces and recommends; it does not execute alone.

No silent failure

Agents must report when they can't do something or hit low confidence. A wrong answer delivered confidently is worse than an honest "I'm not sure."

Brand & compliance respect

Anything user-facing follows Inspirelabs brand guidelines and the platform's content/compliance rules. When unsure, check with your manager before shipping.

Data stays where it belongs

Be deliberate with sensitive data, credentials, internal numbers, merchant terms. Don't pipe them into places they shouldn't go. Ask if unsure.

⚠ The line

"The agent did it automatically" is not an excuse for an irreversible mistake. If you didn't build a checkpoint where one belonged, that's on the build, not the model.

Two Tracks: Internal & External

Everything so far, the value test, the architecture, the agent template, the Wiki, serves work that lands in one of two places. AI Labs builds on two tracks at once, and they carry equal weight: one serves GrabOn, one serves the market. A build can start on either, and the best ones travel between them. Hold both in your head; don't collapse the Labs into "the team that automates GrabOn."

Track A: Internal products

Tools, agents, and dashboards that make Inspirelabs' teams and operations faster and smarter. The customer is any team across the ecosystem, most often a GrabOn team, since that's where the data is deepest; the proving ground is real users, real data, real stakes. This is where most of you will start, because the feedback loop is instant and the data is right here.

Track B: External products

AI products built to stand on their own in the market, a SaaS, a skill, a plugin, an API, an agent someone outside Inspirelabs pays for. These can grow out of a proven internal tool, or be built directly for the world when the opportunity is clearly there. This is how the Labs becomes a venture engine.

How builds travel between tracks

The most powerful pattern is a build that earns its place internally, then crosses to the market, de-risked, because it's already proven on real workflows. But that's a route, not a rule. Some builds are external from day one. Some stay purely internal forever, and that's a complete success. What matters is that every build creates real value somewhere, the value test (§15) is the same on both tracks.

Internal → External (the common route)

Build for a GrabOn team, prove it moves a real number, then ask "who else has this exact problem?" Ship it outward as the lightest form that captures the value. De-risked by reality, we sell what we know works.

External from day one

When the market opportunity is obvious and GrabOn isn't the natural first user, build straight for the world. You still validate against real users and real data, they're just outside the building.

Internal forever

Some tools are too GrabOn-specific to sell, and that's fine. If it saves the company real time or recovers real revenue, it succeeded. Not everything needs a second life.

▲ Example of a build that travels

A stale-coupon agent starts on Track A, internal cleanup for our store pages (recovers CPS, cuts CS load). Proven on GrabOn, the same engine could cross to Track B as a coupon-health product any coupon site or affiliate buys. One build, value on both tracks. Because any internal tool might cross like this, architect for it from the start: config over hardcoding, clean inputs, reusable logic. It costs little extra and keeps the external door open.

The Protocol: How We Communicate

How we build matters; so does how we talk about it, to each other and to the rest of the org. The rule under all four: clarity and proactivity over volume. Say the useful thing early, in a form the reader can act on.

Status before silence

Stuck? Say so early, with what you've already tried. Silence doesn't read as "working hard", it reads as "stuck and hiding." A blocker surfaced early is a blocker someone can clear that day; a blocker sat on for three days is lost time you can't recover.

Proposals, not open questions

Bring "here's what I'd do, and why", not "what do you want me to do?" Even a wrong proposal moves the conversation forward; a blank question hands your thinking to someone with less context than you. The company is actively killing the route-everything-upward habit. Don't rebuild it here.

Respect the room, and the time

Upward communication is deliberate and tight. Lead with the decision or the ask, then the why. The CEO's attention is the scarcest resource in the building: Win The Year exists partly because too much routed through it. Don't add to the queue; clear it.

Write so an agent could read it

Structured, decision-first, unambiguous. It's good for the human reading it now, and it's literally how the Company Brain will consume your work later. Every clear note you write is training data for the system you're building.
